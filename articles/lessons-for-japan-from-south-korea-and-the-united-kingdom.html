Currency Depreciation as Macroeconomic Strategy: Lessons for Japan from South Korea and the United Kingdom

JapanSouth KoreaUnited Kingdom

By M.K · Published August 5, 2026

Abstract

Since early 2022, the Japanese yen has depreciated to its weakest levels in over three decades, driven by a sustained policy divergence between the Bank of Japan's ultra-accommodative monetary stance and the tightening cycles pursued by central banks across the developed world. The depreciation has generated measurable benefits for Japan's export industries and inbound tourism sector while simultaneously eroding domestic household purchasing power through import-driven cost pressures. This paper examines whether Japan's approach constitutes sound macroeconomic strategy by comparing it with South Korea, which pursued a more hawkish monetary response to the same external environment, and the United Kingdom, whose post-Brexit depreciation experience provides a longer-horizon view of the tradeoffs involved. Rather than rendering a single verdict, the analysis maps the conditions under which Japan's weak yen strategy is defensible and those under which its costs become unacceptable — and identifies complementary monetary and fiscal measures that could allow Japan to balance export competitiveness with domestic welfare.

1. Introduction

Exchange rate policy sits at one of the most contested intersections in macroeconomics: the point where decisions made by central banks ripple outward into trade balances, household purchasing power, corporate margins, and the competitive position of entire industries. For most of the postwar period, Japan maintained a moderate and relatively stable yen, with the currency serving as a mechanism of export pricing rather than an active policy instrument. That convention changed sharply from 2022 onwards.

Since early 2022, the Japanese yen has depreciated more than 35% against the US dollar, reaching approximately ¥160 in mid-2024 — its weakest nominal level since 1990. Japan's real effective exchange rate (REER), which adjusts the nominal rate for inflation differentials with trading partners, fell to levels not recorded since the 1970s, indicating a substantial erosion of Japan's international purchasing power that extended well beyond what the nominal figure alone captures (Bank of Japan, 2024). This was not a market accident. The Bank of Japan (BOJ) maintained its near-zero interest rate policy and yield curve control framework while the US Federal Reserve, the European Central Bank, the Bank of England, and most other major central banks raised rates aggressively to combat post-pandemic inflation. The resulting interest rate differential made yen-denominated assets less attractive to global investors, and the currency fell accordingly.

The depreciation has generated both winners and losers within Japan — a distributional split that lies at the heart of the policy debate. Export manufacturers and inbound tourism-facing businesses captured substantial gains; import-dependent households and small businesses absorbed cost pressures. This paper examines the economic consequences of Japan's weak yen policy by tracing its effects through both channels, and then compares Japan's experience with two instructive cases. South Korea, whose economy shares structural similarities with Japan's, pursued a meaningfully different monetary response to the same global rate shock — offering a near-contemporaneous counterfactual. The United Kingdom, whose currency has been under sustained depreciation pressure since the 2016 Brexit referendum, provides a longer-horizon view of what sustained exchange rate weakness looks like when traced through a full economic cycle. The analysis is oriented not toward a simple verdict on Japan's policy, but toward identifying the conditions under which the weak yen strategy is defensible and the complementary fiscal and structural measures that could make it more equitable and sustainable.

2. Background: The 2022 Depreciation and the Bank of Japan's Policy Stance

The Bank of Japan's policy framework leading into 2022 was among the most unconventional of any major central bank. Since 2016, the BOJ had operated a yield curve control (YCC) policy targeting the 10-year Japanese Government Bond yield at approximately zero percent, committing to unlimited bond purchases to enforce that target. The policy rate had been held at -0.1% — a negative rate that effectively penalized banks for holding excess reserves — since 2016. These measures were the culmination of over two decades of effort to lift Japan's chronically deflationary economy toward a sustained 2% inflation target (BOJ, 2022).

When global inflation accelerated in 2022, driven by post-pandemic supply disruptions and the energy price shock following Russia's invasion of Ukraine, the divergence between the BOJ's stance and those of other major central banks created the conditions for sharp yen depreciation. The US Federal Reserve raised its federal funds rate from near-zero to over 5% between March 2022 and July 2023 — the fastest tightening cycle since the 1980s (Federal Reserve, 2023). The Bank of England raised rates from 0.25% to a peak of 5.25%. The Bank of Korea raised its base rate from 0.5% to 3.5% in less than two years. The BOJ, meanwhile, held its policy settings largely unchanged through 2022 and 2023, defending YCC through massive bond purchases that at times consumed the majority of new JGB issuance.

The result was a dramatic widening of interest rate differentials. Capital flowed out of yen-denominated assets in search of higher yields elsewhere, and the yen fell accordingly. Governor Haruhiko Kuroda, in his final months before retiring in April 2023, defended the BOJ's stance on the grounds that Japan's inflation remained cost-push in origin and insufficiently embedded in wage growth to justify rate increases that might choke off a still-fragile economic recovery (BOJ, 2023). His successor, Kazuo Ueda, maintained caution through 2023 before initiating a careful exit from YCC in late 2023, raising the policy rate to 0.25% in July 2024 and to 0.5% in January 2025 — the first meaningful rate increases in seventeen years. These moves signaled a recognition that the domestic costs of sustained depreciation had reached a point requiring policy response, even as the BOJ sought to avoid any sharp yen appreciation that might disrupt the export and tourism gains accumulated since 2022.

3. The Beneficiaries: Export Industries and Inbound Tourism

A weaker yen increases the price competitiveness of Japanese exports by reducing their cost in foreign currency terms, and it lowers the effective cost of travel to Japan for international visitors by increasing the purchasing power of foreign currencies within Japan. Both channels produced substantial and measurable gains between 2022 and 2024.

Japan's export sector — dominated by automobiles, precision electronics, and industrial machinery — benefited directly and significantly. Toyota Motor Corporation reported record operating profits of ¥5.35 trillion in the fiscal year ending March 2024, with company disclosures indicating that each one-yen depreciation against the dollar added approximately ¥45 billion to annual operating income (Toyota Motor Corporation, 2024). For large Japanese manufacturers that price their goods in dollars or euros while incurring the majority of their production costs in yen, the depreciation functions as an automatic margin expansion: revenue in foreign currency translates into more yen when repatriated while yen-denominated costs remain unchanged. Sony, Honda, and Fanuc reported similar currency-linked profit contributions across the same period.

The inbound tourism sector experienced perhaps the most visible transformation. Japan's National Tourism Organization (JNTO) reported that international visitor arrivals reached approximately 25.1 million in 2023, surpassing pre-pandemic 2019 levels, and set a new record of approximately 36.9 million visitors in 2024 (JNTO, 2024). Inbound tourism consumption reached ¥8.14 trillion in 2024, more than triple the pre-pandemic benchmark (Japan Tourism Agency, 2025). The mechanism extended beyond visitor volume: the weak yen substantially increased the real purchasing power of each visitor within Japan, enabling tourists to spend more per trip, stay in higher-tier accommodation, and extend trip durations. Hotel average daily rates in major destinations rose above 2019 benchmarks, and occupancy rates in Tokyo, Kyoto, and Osaka stabilized at 80–90% (Japan Hotel Association, 2024). Tokyo's department store sales exceeded ¥2 trillion in 2024 for the first time on record, driven substantially by foreign visitors purchasing tax-free luxury and branded goods (Japan Department Store Association, 2024).

The economic consequences extended beyond direct tourism and export revenue. Visitor spending flows through local supply chains — restaurants, retail, transport, accommodation — generating employment and tax revenue growth through the multiplier effect. Employment in tourism-adjacent industries in Tokyo expanded significantly alongside revenue growth. In aggregate, the yen depreciation functioned as a de facto stimulus to Japan's service economy, redirecting foreign purchasing power into domestically produced consumption at a scale that direct fiscal transfer programs would struggle to replicate.

4. The Costs: Import Inflation and Household Purchasing Power

The same mechanism that enhanced Japan's export competitiveness and tourism attractiveness imposed significant costs on the domestic economy, primarily through the channel of import-driven inflation. Japan imports the overwhelming majority of its energy — petroleum, liquefied natural gas, and coal — as well as substantial quantities of food, raw materials, and industrial components. A depreciating yen raises the yen-denominated cost of all these imports directly, amplifying global commodity price increases and generating cost pressure independently even when global prices are stable.

Japan's consumer price index rose to 3.2% in January 2023 — the highest reading in over forty years — and remained persistently elevated through 2024, with food prices rising at an even faster rate than the headline figure (Statistics Bureau of Japan, 2024). The BOJ initially characterized this as the domestically generated inflation it had long sought. However, cost-push inflation driven by currency weakness is qualitatively different from demand-pull inflation reflecting genuine domestic economic vitality: it raises prices without a corresponding increase in incomes, functioning as a tax on purchasing power concentrated among households whose consumption is weighted toward necessities.

The most direct measure of this cost was the sustained fall in real wages. Japan's Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare recorded real wages declining on a year-on-year basis for more than 26 consecutive months through 2024, even as nominal wages rose modestly in response to government pressure and labor market tightening (MHLW, 2024). Inflation consistently outpaced nominal wage growth, eroding the purchasing power of ordinary households at a rate that aggregate production figures did not reveal. Lower and middle-income households bore a disproportionate share of this burden; higher-income households and those with exposure to export-sector equities or foreign-currency assets were relatively insulated, or in some cases direct beneficiaries.

Small and medium-sized businesses in import-dependent sectors faced compounded pressure. Food-service businesses dependent on imported ingredients, manufacturers reliant on energy and components, and small retailers on narrow margins saw costs rise through the import channel even as overall sector revenues grew nominally. In Tokyo, the market share of corporately owned food establishments grew relative to individually owned operators, as larger firms with stronger financial positions absorbed the margin compression that smaller businesses could not sustain (Review Co., 2024). The gains of yen depreciation were unevenly distributed: concentrated among large internationally exposed corporations and inbound-facing service industries, diffuse among import-dependent businesses and wage-earning households.

5. South Korea: A Comparable Economy, A Different Policy Choice

South Korea offers one of the most analytically valuable comparisons available for evaluating Japan's approach. The two economies share a structural profile — export-led, manufacturing-intensive, technologically advanced — and both faced the same external interest rate shock in 2022. Both experienced meaningful currency depreciation as a result. The critical difference was the policy response of their respective central banks.

The Korean won depreciated significantly in 2022, falling from approximately ₩1,190 against the US dollar in January to ₩1,440 by October — a decline of roughly 21%. However, the Bank of Korea (BOK) responded in a manner broadly consistent with global central bank behavior: it raised its base rate from 0.5% in August 2021 to 3.5% by January 2023 (Bank of Korea, 2023). The distributional outcomes in each country reflected these policy differences. South Korea experienced a sharper short-term economic deceleration in 2023 as higher borrowing costs weighed on domestic consumption and the housing market. Household debt — among the highest in the OECD relative to GDP — became more expensive to service, and the construction sector faced stress. These were real costs of the BOK's hawkish stance. However, the won's partial stabilization meant South Korean households faced a significantly less severe import inflation shock: energy and food costs rose, but the currency channel amplified them substantially less than in Japan. Real wage erosion was less pronounced and shorter-lived.

South Korea's export sector — dominated by semiconductors, shipbuilding, and automobiles — saw results that were substantially shaped by industry-specific rather than currency factors. Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix faced a severe cyclical downturn in global semiconductor demand in 2022 and 2023 that depressed revenues regardless of exchange rate conditions. When the semiconductor cycle recovered sharply in 2024, Korean exporters benefited from improved unit economics alongside a won that had partially recovered (OECD, 2024). This pattern suggests that for South Korea's most important export industry, currency depreciation was neither the decisive competitive variable nor the critical risk — a structural observation with implications for Japan's automobile and electronics exporters as well.

The South Korean case does not represent an unambiguous argument against Japan's approach. The BOK's rate increases carried genuine domestic costs, and Japan's deflationary history created reasonable grounds for greater caution about premature tightening. However, the comparison establishes an important point: a more moderate depreciation path was available to Japan, pursued successfully by a structurally comparable economy, and the divergence in outcomes between Japanese and South Korean households through 2022–2024 reflects a genuine policy choice with distributional consequences.

6. The United Kingdom: Currency Depreciation Over a Longer Time Horizon

The United Kingdom offers a different kind of comparison: not a contemporaneous alternative policy response, but a longer-run view of what sustained exchange rate weakness looks like when its consequences are traced through a full economic cycle. The pound sterling entered a period of structural depreciation following the 2016 Brexit referendum, providing nearly a decade of data on how currency weakness interacts with trade patterns, inflation, household welfare, and monetary policy over time.

Following the June 2016 referendum result, the pound fell from approximately $1.48 against the US dollar to $1.20 within months — a rapid depreciation of roughly 19% driven by market repricing of future UK trade relationships and institutional uncertainty. The Bank of England, after a brief precautionary rate cut in August 2016, gradually moved back toward rate normalization before embarking on an aggressive tightening cycle in 2022–2023, raising rates from 0.1% to a peak of 5.25% by August 2023 as UK CPI inflation exceeded 11% — the highest in the G7 (Bank of England, 2023; ONS, 2023). Despite this aggressive tightening, the pound remained substantially weaker than its pre-referendum level throughout the period.

The UK's tourism sector experienced measurable gains from the weaker pound. International visitor arrivals and visitor spending in the UK increased as sterling fell, making London and other destinations more affordable for American, European, and Asian tourists. High-end retail and hospitality in London benefited from the elevated purchasing power of foreign visitors. These dynamics parallel what Japan has experienced since 2022, and they were real: sterling weakness contributed to a sustained pricing advantage for UK-based tourism and services catering to foreign visitors (VisitBritain, 2023).

However, the UK's broader economic experience exposes the limits of exchange rate depreciation as a sustained economic strategy. UK manufacturing — the sector theoretically most positioned to benefit from export competitiveness — showed disappointing export volume growth despite the currency advantage. UK goods export volumes failed to surge proportionally to the depreciation, in part because UK supply chains were already deeply integrated with EU partners who now faced additional friction from Brexit-related trade barriers. The currency competitiveness gain was partially or substantially offset by new non-tariff costs, delayed investment, and supply chain fragmentation (Springford, 2022). This is a structural caution for any country assuming that currency pricing advantages translate directly into export volume gains without regard to the configuration of trade relationships and supply chains.

The more lasting UK lesson concerns the distributional and fiscal consequences of sustained depreciation. UK households experienced persistent above-target inflation through the late 2010s and into the 2020s, compressing real living standards particularly for lower-income deciles. The government was ultimately compelled to introduce direct fiscal interventions — energy price caps in 2022 and 2023, targeted cost-of-living transfers — to partially offset the household costs that the weak pound had helped generate. The constituencies that benefited from depreciation (export manufacturers, inbound tourism businesses, holders of foreign-currency assets) differed systematically from those who bore the costs (import-dependent households, SMEs, workers in non-tradeable sectors), and the distributional gap required fiscal correction. For Japan, whose tourism tax revenues have grown substantially since 2022, the UK pattern argues strongly for actively redistributing those gains toward domestic household welfare rather than treating the tourism boom as a self-contained success.

Japan's BOJ was not wrong to prioritize escaping deflation before tightening. Japan's deflationary trap was genuine, persistent, and economically costly over three decades, and premature tightening carried real risks of re-entrenchment. Viewed through the lens of tourism performance, export profitability, and aggregate production figures, the weak yen generated substantial economic activity that would not have occurred under a tighter monetary stance. The South Korea comparison shows that a more hawkish policy was available; it also shows that it carried real domestic costs that a still-fragile Japanese recovery might not have absorbed without significant disruption.

At the same time, the evidence from Japan's domestic economy — sustained real wage declines, subdued household consumption, and SME margin compression — and the UK's decade-long experience suggest that yen depreciation has functioned as a sector-selective stimulus: highly beneficial for internationally exposed industries, costly for domestically oriented households and businesses. The gains have been concentrated; the costs have been diffuse. This asymmetry is structurally common to exchange rate depreciation strategies but is consistently underweighted in policy discussions, which tend to foreground aggregate production figures while underweighting distributional outcomes.

The UK experience adds a further caution: a currency weak enough to stimulate tourism and exports for long enough eventually embeds imported inflation expectations that require active policy intervention to unwind — a process the Bank of England's aggressive tightening cycle is still navigating. South Korea's earlier tightening has, to date, avoided this dynamic, though at the cost of slower domestic recovery in 2023. Japan's belated rate normalization — begun in 2024 — represents a recognition that the weak yen's costs have accumulated to a point requiring a policy response, even if the normalization trajectory must remain gradual.

8. Recommendations: Conditional Policy Paths

The comparative evidence supports conditional recommendations aligned with competing but legitimate policy objectives. Rather than a single prescription, the analysis identifies two orientations and a hybrid path that draws on the strengths of both.

If the primary objective is sustaining export competitiveness and inbound tourism revenue, the BOJ's gradual normalization path — raising rates carefully while avoiding sharp yen appreciation — is broadly appropriate. Complete elimination of yen weakness would remove the price competitiveness advantage that has driven the documented inbound tourism and export gains. A yen recovering abruptly to pre-2022 levels would compress hotel revenues, reduce foreign visitor spending power, and cut directly into the export margins that supported record corporate profits in 2023 and 2024. Under this objective, the complementary priority should be actively investing the substantial tourism-linked tax revenues that have grown alongside the boom into supply-side infrastructure: accommodation capacity expansion, multilingual public services, and geographic dispersal programs to reduce overtourism pressure in congested districts and share benefits more broadly across regions.

If the primary objective is protecting household welfare and ensuring that economic growth translates into improved living standards, a more active normalization of monetary policy is warranted. The BOJ should continue its rate normalization on a transparent and credible schedule, while the government deploys direct fiscal support to lower and middle-income households to offset the accumulated import inflation burden. South Korea's approach — rate normalization combined with targeted household support — is closer to the right model under this objective. The domestic data suggests that a trajectory of nominal production growth alongside eroding real household purchasing power is not sustainable as a political or social proposition over the medium term.

The most constructive path available treats these objectives as requiring balance rather than resolution. The BOJ's gradual rate normalization moves in the right direction and should continue. What would complement it is a more deliberate fiscal strategy: reinvesting tourism-sector tax revenues into domestic welfare programs that offset the import inflation burden borne by households, alongside active policies to shift inbound tourism growth toward higher-value visitors rather than maximizing volume. The UK experience and Japan's emerging overtourism data — with resident surveys in major tourism districts reporting growing congestion concerns — suggest that quantity-maximizing approaches eventually generate negative externalities that erode the competitive advantage they were intended to sustain.

Ultimately, the South Korean and UK experiences both suggest that exchange rate policy is not merely a monetary variable but a distributional instrument. Its full consequences — for households, businesses, and fiscal balance — require a response spanning both monetary and fiscal domains, calibrated to the specific demographic, industrial, and institutional context of each country. For Japan, the next phase of policy design should reflect this: the yen depreciation era has produced measurable gains, and the task now is ensuring that those gains are distributed more equitably and that the structural risks — overtourism, household purchasing power, SME fragility — are addressed before they become self-correcting in ways that are difficult to manage.
